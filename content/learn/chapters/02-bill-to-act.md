## What is a bill?

A {{bill}} is a written plan for a new law. It can also be a plan to change a law we already have. [[f:bill-definition]]

A bill is not a law yet. First it must pass both houses of Parliament. Both houses must agree to exactly the same words. [[f:bill-identical-both-houses]]

Then the {{Governor-General}} signs it. Now it is an {{Act}}, which is a law. [[f:royal-assent-final-step]] [[f:becomes-act]]

---screen---

## Where ideas come from

Ideas for new laws come from many places. [[f:bill-idea-sources]]

A government department may spot a problem and tell its minister. A political party may want to act on its ideas. Community groups and businesses can talk to MPs about what they want changed. A committee may look into an issue and suggest a new law. [[f:bill-idea-sources]]

Most bills are brought in by government ministers. [[f:most-bills-ministers]]

---screen---

## Two kinds of bills

Most bills are government bills. A minister brings them in. [[f:most-bills-ministers]]

Any other MP or senator can bring in a bill too. This is a {{private member's bill}}. [[f:private-bills]]

These bills rarely pass. Since 1901, only about 30 have passed. [[f:private-bills-30]] One reason is time. Parliament spends most of its time on the government's business. Another reason is numbers. The government usually has most of the seats in the House. [[f:private-bills-why]]

---screen---

## Which house goes first?

A bill starts in the house where the person bringing it in sits. [[f:bill-starts-where-sponsor-sits]]

Most ministers sit in the {{House of Representatives}}. So most bills start there. [[f:most-bills-start-house]]

Some bills must start in the House. A bill that sets a tax, or spends public money, cannot start in the {{Senate}}. [[f:money-bills-start-house]]

---screen---

## First reading

The first step is the {{first reading}}. The bill is brought into the house and its title is read out for the first time. [[f:first-reading]]

The Clerk is the top official of each house. [[f:clerk-def]] The Clerk reads the title three times as the bill moves through each house. Each reading means the bill has passed another stage. [[f:three-readings]]

On OPAX, a bill page lists each stage recorded for the bill, with its date. A stage can appear once for each day it was debated. The list usually starts with "Introduced". [[f:opax-recorded-stages]]

---screen---

## Second reading

Next comes the {{second reading}}. The member in charge of the bill, usually a minister, asks that it "now be read a second time". [[f:second-reading-motion]]

Then members debate the main idea of the bill. They say why they support it or oppose it. [[f:second-reading-debate]]

After the debate, they vote. If the vote passes, the house agrees with the main idea. The bill can then move on, and changes can be looked at. [[f:second-reading-vote]]

---screen---

## The Federation Chamber

The House has a second room for debate. It is called the {{Federation Chamber}}. It can meet at the same time as the House, and any member of the House can go. [[f:fed-chamber-what]]

It is used for bills that most members are expected to agree on. [[f:fed-chamber-use]]

No {{division}}, or formal counted vote, can be held there. Its decisions must be confirmed by the House. [[f:fed-chamber-no-divisions]] [[f:fed-chamber-confirm]]

---screen---

## Committees take a closer look

Either house can send a bill to a {{committee}} for a closer look. [[f:committee-send-bill]]

A committee is a small group of MPs or senators. Most have 6 to 10 members. [[f:committee-size]]

Committees ask people and groups to send in their views. These are called submissions. Then they hold hearings to ask more questions. [[f:committee-submissions-hearings]]

At the end, the committee writes a report. It may suggest changes. [[f:committee-report]]

---screen---

## Senate inquiries

The Senate can send a bill to one of its committees. This is a {{Senate inquiry}}. It can even happen while the bill is still in the House. [[f:senate-referral-early]]

Committees look into issues as well as bills. [[f:committee-issues-and-bills]]

Members can use the report to decide how to vote on a bill, or to write changes to it. [[f:committee-report-use]]

---screen---

## Consideration in detail

After the second reading, members can go through the bill part by part. [[f:cid-optional]]

In the House, this is called {{consideration in detail}}. In the Senate, it is called committee of the whole. It is not one of the small committees. It is a stage where senators discuss the bill in detail. [[f:consideration-in-detail]]

This is where most changes, called {{amendments|amendment}}, are dealt with. Members can speak about each amendment and vote for or against it. [[f:amendments-when]]

This stage is optional. [[f:cid-optional]]

---screen---

## Who can change a bill?

Any MP or senator can suggest an amendment. This is called "moving an amendment". [[f:amend-anyone]]

The amendment must be written down and given to the Clerk. The Clerk shares copies so everyone can read it. [[f:amend-written]]

Then members debate it and vote. If most agree, the bill is changed. [[f:amend-majority]]

Opposition and crossbench members suggest changes most often. That is because most bills come from the government. [[f:amend-who-most]]

---screen---

## Third reading

At the {{third reading}}, members vote on the bill in its final form. If it passes, that house has passed the bill. [[f:third-reading]]

When the House passes a bill, the Speaker sends a formal note to the Senate. It is called a message. It asks the Senate to agree to the bill. [[f:house-message-to-senate]]

---screen---

## The other house

In the second house, the bill goes through the same steps again. There is a first reading, a second reading, optional committee stages and a third reading. [[f:senate-stages]]

When a bill arrives, the Senate can debate it straight away. It can send it to a committee first, or set a later day for debate. [[f:senate-options]]

---screen---

## Going back and forth

What if the second house changes the bill? Both houses must agree to every change. [[f:amendments-both-houses]]

Then the first house must agree to those changes too. [[f:amendment-shuttle]] You can see this in the last example at the end of this chapter.

The bill can only go on when both houses agree to the same words. [[f:bill-identical-both-houses]]

---screen---

## When the houses disagree

If one house will not pass a bill, it cannot become law. [[f:not-passed-not-law]]

The house where it started can pass it again and send it back. It can change the bill, or it can put it aside. [[f:not-passed-options]]

For a bill that started in the House, there is one more path. It is set out in the Constitution. [[f:double-dissolution-house-bills]] See the next screen.

---screen---

## Double dissolution

Say the House passes a bill and the Senate blocks it. After three months, the House passes it again, and the Senate blocks it again. [[f:double-dissolution-steps]]

Then the Prime Minister can ask the Governor-General to close both houses at once, so every seat goes to an election. This is a {{double dissolution}}. [[f:double-dissolution-election]]

It has happened 7 times. [[f:double-dissolution-7]]

---screen---

## After a double dissolution

After the election, the new House can pass the bill again. If the Senate still blocks it, all senators and members can meet together and vote on it as one group. [[f:joint-sitting]]

This is called a joint sitting. It has happened only once, in 1974. [[f:joint-sitting]] [[f:joint-sitting-1974]]

---screen---

## Royal assent

When both houses have passed the same bill, it goes to the Governor-General. Signing it is called {{royal assent}}. It is the final step in making a law. [[f:royal-assent-final-step]]

The Governor-General can send a bill back with suggested changes. [[f:gg-return-bill]] But no Governor-General has ever refused to sign a bill. [[f:gg-never-refused]]

Once signed, the bill becomes an Act of Parliament. [[f:becomes-act]]

---screen---

## When does the law start?

An Act does not always start working on the day it is signed. The day it starts is called its {{commencement}}. [[f:commencement-28-days]]

An Act starts on the date written in it. If it gives no date, it starts 28 days after royal assent. [[f:commencement-28-days]]

On OPAX, stage dates show a bill's progress through Parliament. They do not tell you when a law starts. [[f:opax-stage-dates-not-commencement]]

---screen---

## Money bills

Some bills are about tax or spending. These are {{money bills|money bill}}. [[f:money-bill-def]] The Budget is passed as a set of these bills. [[f:budget-bills]]

The Senate cannot change a bill that sets a tax. It also cannot change the bill that pays for the government's ordinary yearly services. [[f:s53-no-amend]]

But it can send the bill back to the House and ask for changes. The House decides whether to make them. [[f:s53-request]]

---screen---

## Rules made under an Act

Many Acts set out the big picture. They can give a minister the power to make the detailed rules. [[f:delegated-purpose]]

These rules are called {{delegated legislation}}. Regulations are one kind. [[f:delegated-what]]

They have the same force as a law made by Parliament. But they can be made or changed without a debate and vote in Parliament. [[f:delegated-same-force]] [[f:delegated-no-debate]]

Each one must go on a public register before it can take effect. Unless it says otherwise, it starts the day after it is registered. [[f:delegated-register-6-days]] [[f:delegated-commence-s12]]

---screen---

## Disallowance

Parliament still keeps watch over these rules. A {{sitting day}} is a day a house meets. [[f:sitting-def]] Each rule must be shown to Parliament within 6 sitting days. [[f:delegated-register-6-days]]

Within 15 sitting days, an MP or senator can ask their house to overrule it. This is {{disallowance}}. The house then has 15 sitting days to decide. If it does not decide, the rule is overruled. [[f:disallow-15-days]]

Some rules cannot be overruled. [[f:delegated-some-not]]

---screen---

## Bills that run out of time

Before an election, the session of Parliament is formally closed. [[f:prorogation-def]] Any bills still waiting then expire. They can be brought in again in the next Parliament. [[f:prorogued-bills-expire]]

OPAX shows each bill's status from Parliament's bill records. One you will often see is {{lapsed}}. A bill marked lapsed did not become law. The same bill can be brought in again later, and some then pass. [[f:opax-lapsed-status]] [[f:prorogued-bills-expire]]

Most bills take 2 to 3 months to pass, but some take years. About 200 bills come to Parliament each year. [[f:bill-time]] [[f:bills-per-year]]
