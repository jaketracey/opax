## Parliament has two houses

Australia's Parliament has three parts. There are two houses, the {{House of Representatives}} and the {{Senate}}. The third part is the King, who is represented by the {{Governor-General}}. [[f:parliament-parts]]

One of Parliament's main jobs is to make laws for the whole country. [[f:parliament-makes-laws]]

A proposed law must pass both houses before it can become law. [[f:bill-identical-both-houses]]

---screen---

## MPs and electorates

The House of Representatives has 150 members. [[f:house-150]]

Each member represents an area called an {{electorate}}. People also call it a seat. [[f:electorate-def]] Within each state, electorates have about the same number of voters. [[f:electorate-equal-voters]]

A person elected to the House is called a {{member of parliament}}, or MP. [[f:member-mp]]

Elections for the House are held at least every 3 years. [[f:house-term]]

---screen---

## Senators and states

The Senate has 76 {{senators|senator}}. Each state has 12 senators. The Northern Territory and the ACT have 2 each. [[f:senate-76]]

A senator represents a whole state or territory, not one electorate. [[f:senator-represents-state]]

State senators serve 6-year terms. Every 3 years, half of them face an election. Territory senators serve 3-year terms. [[f:senate-terms]]

---screen---

## Why those numbers?

The Constitution says the House should have about twice as many members as the Senate. [[f:s24-twice]]

Each state gets House seats based on how many people live there. [[f:s24-population]]

Electorate lines are redrawn from time to time. The aim is to keep the number of voters in each electorate close to the average for its state or territory. [[f:aec-redistribution-aim]]

---screen---

## The numbers right now

As at 28 May 2026, the House had 150 members. The government had 94. The opposition had 41. Minor parties and independents had 15. [[f:house-numbers-2026]]

As at 15 May 2026, the Senate had 76 senators. The government had 30. The opposition had 27. Minor parties and independents had 19. [[f:senate-numbers-2026]]

The AEC has said the House will still have 150 seats at the next election. [[f:house-150]] The other numbers can change, so always check the date.

---screen---

## The government

The {{government}} is the party, or group of parties, with the support of most members of the House. [[f:government-def]] A group of parties that work together like this is called a coalition. [[f:coalition-def]]

The government has members in the Senate too. But it may not have most of the seats there. [[f:gov-senate-maybe]] In fact, a government with most of the Senate seats is unusual. [[f:gov-senate-unusual]]

---screen---

## The Prime Minister

The {{Prime Minister}} leads the government. [[f:pm-leads]]

Australians do not vote for the Prime Minister directly. The members of the party in government choose their leader. [[f:pm-chosen]]

By tradition, the Prime Minister sits in the House, not the Senate. [[f:pm-house]]

The job is not mentioned in the Constitution. [[f:pm-not-constitution]]

---screen---

## Ministers

The Prime Minister picks some government MPs and senators to be {{ministers|minister}}. [[f:pm-picks-ministers]]

Each minister looks after an area, such as health or defence. That area is called a portfolio. [[f:minister-portfolio]]

There are usually about 20 ministers in the House and 10 in the Senate. [[f:minister-numbers]]

A minister must be an MP or senator, or become one within 3 months. [[f:s64-ministers-in-parliament]]

---screen---

## Cabinet

The top ministers form {{Cabinet}}. It is made up of the Prime Minister and about 20 ministers. [[f:cabinet-def]]

Cabinet meets often, sometimes each week. It sets the plans of the government and makes big choices for the nation. [[f:cabinet-meets]]

Once Cabinet makes a choice, all its members are expected to back it in public. [[f:cabinet-solidarity]]

---screen---

## The opposition

The {{opposition}} is the biggest party, or group of parties, that is not in power. [[f:opposition-def]] It is sometimes called the alternative government, because it could take over. [[f:opposition-alternative]]

Part of its job is to check the work of those in power and to put forward other plans. [[f:opposition-role]]

The Leader of the Opposition picks {{shadow ministers|shadow minister}}. Each one keeps a close eye on one minister. [[f:shadow-ministers]]

---screen---

## The crossbench

Some MPs and senators are not in the government or the opposition. Some belong to minor parties. Others are independents, who belong to no party. [[f:independent-def]]

They sit on the {{crossbench}}. [[f:crossbench-def]]

When the government does not have most of the Senate seats, crossbench senators can decide if a bill passes or fails. This is called the balance of power. [[f:balance-of-power]]

---screen---

## The Speaker and the President

Each house has someone who runs its meetings. In the House it is the {{Speaker}}. In the Senate it is the {{President of the Senate}}. [[f:presiding-officers]]

They call on people to speak, keep order and run votes. [[f:po-duties]]

Members of the House vote to choose the Speaker after each election. Senators elect the President. [[f:po-chosen]]

---screen---

## Whips

Parties choose a {{whip}} to help them work as a team. [[f:whip-def]] Some small parties do not have one. [[f:whip-small-parties]]

Whips make sure members turn up and vote together. They count the votes in a division. They also arrange pairs, so the numbers stay balanced when someone is away. [[f:whip-duties]]

Major parties usually have a chief whip and 2 deputy whips. [[f:whip-numbers]]

---screen---

## The Governor-General

The Governor-General represents the King in Australia. [[f:gg-represents-king]]

The King appoints the Governor-General on the Prime Minister's advice. [[f:gg-appointed]]

By tradition, the Governor-General acts on the advice of the government. [[f:gg-advice]]

One key job is to sign bills that both houses have passed. This is called royal assent. [[f:royal-assent-gg]]

---screen---

## One word, two meanings

The AEC is the body that runs our national elections. AEC is short for the Australian Electoral Commission. [[f:aec-def]] On its website, it calls each electorate an "electoral division". [[f:aec-electoral-division]]

But inside Parliament, a {{division}} is a vote where each name is written down. [[f:division-def]]

In this guide, "division" always means a vote. When we mean a place, we say electorate or seat.
