## Three levels of government

Australia has {{three levels of government}}. There is the federal Parliament in Canberra. There are parliaments for each state and territory. And there are local councils. [[f:three-levels]]

There are more than 500 local councils around Australia. [[f:councils-550]]

Each level looks after different things. Sometimes their jobs overlap. [[f:levels-overlap]]

---screen---

## How it began

Before 1901, the states were British colonies, each with its own parliament. [[f:colonies]]

In 1901 they joined together to form one nation, the Commonwealth of Australia. This is called {{federation}}. They kept their own constitutions and parliaments. [[f:federation-1901]] [[f:federation-def]]

The Australian Constitution sets out how power is split between the federal level and the states. [[f:constitution-splits-power]]

---screen---

## What the federal Parliament can do

The Constitution lists the things the federal Parliament can make laws about. [[f:s51-list]]

Two parts of it, sections 51 and 52, list these areas. [[f:s51-list]] Section 51 includes trade with other countries, tax, defence and currency (our coins and notes). It also covers our dealings with other nations. [[f:s51-heads]]

These are things that affect the whole country. [[f:federal-whole-country]]

---screen---

## Exclusive powers

Some powers belong only to the federal Parliament. They are called {{exclusive powers}}. The states cannot make laws about them. [[f:exclusive-def]]

Section 52 is one example. Only the federal Parliament can make laws about the federal public service. [[f:s52-example]]

Customs duties are another example. Only the federal Parliament can make laws about them. [[f:customs-s90]]

---screen---

## What the states look after

Some powers were not given to the federal Parliament. They stay with the states. These are called {{residual powers}}. [[f:residual-def]]

Section 107 says the states keep the powers they had as colonies. The only ones they lose are those the Constitution takes away. [[f:s107]]

State parliaments make laws about things like schools, hospitals, roads, public transport and mining. [[f:state-areas]]

---screen---

## Shared powers

Some powers can be used by both the federal Parliament and the states. These are {{concurrent powers}}. [[f:concurrent-def]]

For example, both can make laws about the environment, marriage and tax. [[f:concurrent-examples]]

Education, health and water are also shared. [[f:shared-education-health]] So both levels can have laws on the same topic. [[f:concurrent-def]]

---screen---

## When laws clash

What if a state law and a federal law clash? Section 109 of the Constitution decides. [[f:s109-text]]

The federal law wins. The state law has no effect, but only for the part that clashes. [[f:s109-text]] [[f:s109-plain]]

If people disagree about who can make a law, the {{High Court}} decides. [[f:high-court-decides]]

---screen---

## Handing over a power

A state can hand a power over to the federal Parliament. [[f:referral]]

In 2009, the federal Parliament passed the Fair Work Act. [[f:fair-work-act-2009]] That year, every state except Western Australia handed over its workplace power. [[f:referral-2009]]

Most Australian workers are now in one national system. [[f:referral-2009]]

---screen---

## State parliaments

There are 6 state parliaments and 2 territory parliaments. They meet in the capital city of each state or territory. [[f:state-parliaments-count]]

Each state has its own constitution. It sets out the powers of its {{state parliament}}. [[f:state-constitution]]

The laws a state parliament makes apply within that state. [[f:state-laws-within]]

---screen---

## One house or two

Victoria, South Australia and Tasmania each have a parliament with two houses. [[f:houses-vic]] [[f:houses-sa]] [[f:houses-tas]]

In Victoria, the two houses are the Legislative Assembly and the Legislative Council. [[f:houses-vic]]

In South Australia and Tasmania, the two houses are the House of Assembly and the Legislative Council. [[f:houses-sa]] [[f:houses-tas]]

This guide does not yet say how the other parliaments are set up. To find out, check each parliament's own website.

---screen---

## Territories

The territories do not have their own constitutions. [[f:territories-no-constitution]]

Instead, section 122 lets the federal Parliament make laws for them. It used this power to set up a parliament for the NT and one for the ACT. Each is called a {{legislative assembly}}. [[f:s122-territories]]

The federal Parliament can undo a territory law, but this rarely happens. [[f:territory-override]]

---screen---

## Local councils

The states and territories can make laws to set up local councils. [[f:councils-created]]

Councils look after things like local roads, parks, rubbish collection and libraries. [[f:council-jobs]]

The ACT does not have local councils. Its Legislative Assembly does the jobs of both a state and a council. [[f:act-no-councils]]
