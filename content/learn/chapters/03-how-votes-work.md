## Most decisions use voices

Parliament makes decisions by majority vote. [[f:majority-vote]]

Most decisions are made {{on the voices}}. [[f:voices-most]] The Speaker or President asks those in favour to say "aye" and those against to say "no". Then they decide which side has more support and announce the result. [[f:voices-how]]

"Aye" is the word for yes in Parliament. [[f:aye-def]]

---screen---

## Calling a division

If more than one member challenges the result, a {{division}} is held. [[f:division-challenge]] A division is a formal vote where each person's name is recorded. [[f:division-def]]

Bells ring all through Parliament House, usually for 4 minutes. Then the doors are locked. Members who arrive late are not allowed in. [[f:division-bells]]

---screen---

## Ayes and noes

In a division, members who vote yes sit to the Speaker's right. Members who vote no sit to the Speaker's left. In the Senate, it is the President's right and left. [[f:division-sides]]

The votes are counted and the names are written down. Then the result is announced. [[f:division-recorded-announced]]

A division can only count if enough members are there. In the Senate, at least 19 senators must vote. In the House, 30 members must be present. [[f:quorum-numbers]]

---screen---

## Tied votes

In the House, the Speaker does not vote unless the votes are tied. Then the Speaker has the deciding vote. [[f:speaker-casting]]

The President of the Senate can always vote, like any other senator. This keeps every state's say equal. [[f:president-votes]]

If a Senate vote is tied, the proposal fails. [[f:senate-tie]]

---screen---

## Divisions on OPAX

On OPAX, each division has its own page. [[f:opax-division-page]]

At the top you see the result, the number of ayes and noes, the house and the date. Next comes the question that was put to the vote. Then you see how each party voted and every member's name. [[f:opax-division-sections]]

Only a division leaves a record of each member's vote. [[f:opax-voices-no-record]]

---screen---

## Reading the result

OPAX shows whether the proposal was agreed to or not. [[f:opax-outcome-words]]

On the website it says "Agreed" or "Not agreed". In the app it says "Agreed to" or "Negatived", which means it failed. Some pages use the record's own words: "affirmative" means agreed, and "negative" means not agreed. [[f:opax-outcome-words]]

All of these say the same thing: did more members vote aye or no?

---screen---

## Not every vote is on the whole bill

A bill can have many divisions. Some are about amendments. Some are about how the house runs its business, such as whether to end a debate. [[f:opax-procedural]]

OPAX calls that second kind procedural votes. It counts them apart from the others. [[f:opax-procedural]]

A division on an amendment is not a vote on the bill itself. [[f:opax-amendment-note]] To find a vote on the whole bill, look for the second or third reading.

---screen---

## When a bill has no divisions

Many bills pass without a single division. On OPAX, more than half of the federal bills that passed have none recorded. [[f:opax-passed-no-divisions]]

Remember, most questions are decided on the voices. [[f:voices-most]]

So if OPAX shows no divisions for a bill, it does not mean everyone agreed. It just means nobody's vote was recorded by name. [[f:opax-no-divisions]]

---screen---

## Pairs

Sometimes an MP has to be away for a vote. A member on the other side can agree not to vote, to balance it out. This is called a {{pair}}. [[f:pair-def]]

Party whips arrange pairs, so the numbers stay balanced. [[f:whip-duties]]

On the OPAX website, paired members are listed under "Paired, not counted". They are not part of the ayes or noes. [[f:opax-paired]]

---screen---

## Voting as a team

Members of a party usually vote as a team, all the same way. [[f:party-team]] This guide calls that voting on {{party lines|party line}}.

Parties often have rules about voting as a team. [[f:party-rules]] Whips make sure members know how to vote. [[f:whip-how-to-vote]]

Working together gives members more power than acting alone. [[f:team-power]]

---screen---

## Crossing the floor

Sometimes a member votes against the rest of their party in a division. They walk across the room to sit with the other side. This is called {{crossing the floor}}. [[f:crossing-def]]

It is rare. [[f:crossing-rare]]

A member might do it because of strong views, the people they represent, or their conscience. [[f:crossing-why]] It can cost them support in their party. [[f:crossing-cost]]

---screen---

## How OPAX spots it

You can ask OPAX who crossed the floor in a division. To answer, it compares each member's vote with how most of their party voted. [[f:opax-crossing-rule]]

It only checks parties with at least 5 aye or no votes. It leaves out independents, ties, pairs and absent members. [[f:opax-crossing-rule]]

Party labels can sometimes show a later party. So OPAX's list is a guide, not a final record. [[f:opax-crossing-caveat]]

---screen---

## Conscience votes

Sometimes a party lets its members vote by their own beliefs. This is a {{conscience vote}}, also called a free vote. [[f:free-vote-def]] Each party decides when to allow one. [[f:free-vote-party-decides]]

Free votes are rare. From 1950 to 2021, only 43 votes were free votes. [[f:free-vote-43]]

Most have been on issues like euthanasia, stem cell research and same-sex marriage. [[f:free-vote-topics]]

---screen---

## Spotting a conscience vote

In a conscience vote, members of the same party may vote different ways. [[f:free-vote-def]]

On OPAX, look at how each party voted. Each party shows its ayes and its noes. [[f:opax-party-split]] If a big party has members on both sides, that is a clue.

But a clue is not proof. A split can also mean some members crossed the floor. Read the question and its notes to be sure.

---screen---
