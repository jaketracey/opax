Press kit

OPAX makes the public record of Australian politics searchable and citable: what every MP said, how they voted, who funds the parties and where public money goes, in one place, with every item linked to its official source. Free, open source, non-partisan. This page has what a newsroom needs to describe it, check it and credit it.

In one line

Ask what Australian politicians actually said, see how they voted, and follow who funds them.

Boilerplate

Short. OPAX (opax.com.au) is a free, open-source site and app that makes the Australian parliamentary record searchable and citable: speeches, votes, bills, donations, grants and contracts, each linked to its official source.

Long. OPAX, the Open Parliamentary Accountability Exchange, puts the public record of Australian politics in one place. It holds Hansard speeches from the Commonwealth Parliament and the New South Wales, Victorian, South Australian, Queensland and ACT legislatures, every recorded federal division since 2013 with each member's vote, every federal bill since 2013 with a plain-English summary marked as machine-written, political donation returns from the federal and state electoral commissions with donors named as the commissions publish them, and federal contracts and grants. Every item links to its official source. Ask answers questions in plain English from retrieved passages only, and cites them. A read-only MCP service lets research tools query the same record. OPAX is independent and non-partisan, built in Melbourne by Jake Tracey and published by Noice Pty Ltd under an open-source licence (AGPL-3.0).

What you can do with it

  • Search the record by keyword, speaker, party, state, topic and year, and export results as CSV, BibTeX or RIS. Search.
  • Ask a question and get an answer written only from retrieved records, each passage cited. When the record is thin, the answer says so. Ask.
  • Follow the money on a map of donors, industries and parties, beside the grants and contracts that flow back. Recorded connections are shown side by side, never summed into a verdict. Money.
  • Read a bill in plain English: what it changes, who sponsored it, how the parties divided and the speeches that argued it. Bills.
  • Query by machine through a read-only MCP service for research tools and AI assistants, with a key from a free account. Reference.

The Victorian election hub covers the 28 November 2026 election. The election page covers all 88 districts and 8 regions: published member records, previous winning margins from the VEC's final count, and the member's Victorian Hansard speeches and 2026 division records, each linked to the official record. How to cite OPAX

Cite the official record and credit OPAX for the finding. Every OPAX page shows the source it came from, so a reader can check it without us.

  • A speech: Hansard, House of Representatives, 12 February 2025, via OPAX (opax.com.au), with the link to the OPAX record page.
  • A vote: Division on the [bill name], Senate, 27 March 2025, via OPAX.
  • A donation: AEC Transparency Register, 2024-25 annual return, via OPAX.
  • An answer from Ask: quote and cite the records the answer cites, not the answer itself. Ask writes from retrieved passages and can miss context.
  • A chart or table from OPAX: "Source: official records compiled by OPAX, opax.com.au", with the page link.

Citation formats, known limits and the collection method are on the Methods page. Bill summaries and topic labels are machine-written and marked as such; the official text is one link away.

Independence and corrections

OPAX takes no money from parties, candidates or campaigns and carries no advertising. It publishes no polls, predictions, scores or rankings, and it does not characterise individual politicians. Recorded connections do not establish influence. The data belongs to the public bodies that published it; each page names its source and, where one applies, its licence. The About page sets out the licences and what is kept off the public site.

If a record is wrong, email support@opax.com.au or open an issue on GitHub. Corrections to the data are the thing we most want to hear about.

Who makes it

OPAX is built by Jake Tracey in Melbourne and published by Noice Pty Ltd. The code is open source under AGPL-3.0 at github.com/jaketracey/opax. OPAX is not affiliated with any parliament, government agency or political party.
